Episode I · History

From Clockwork Automata to ASIMO and HUBO

Half a century of chasing the dream of a walking machine. How a story that began in a Tokyo laboratory ended in a victory on a California field.

Published 2026.06.20 · 14 min read · by Lucky Please Editorial
Prologue

The Old Dream of a Walking Machine

Since 2025, humanoid robots have made the news almost every week. Tesla's Optimus walks a factory floor, Figure's robot steps onto a BMW line, and a machine from China's Unitree dances on a stage. It can look like a technology that appeared out of nowhere. Yet the history of human-shaped machines standing and walking on two legs reaches back half a century. To read today's boom correctly, you first have to know that long lineage.

The desire to build a machine in our own image is older than electricity, older than the computer. In eighteenth-century Europe, clockmakers built dolls that wrote letters and struck keys using nothing but wound springs. Jaquet-Droz's "Writer" dipped a quill in ink and composed a sentence one character at a time. In Japan, around the same period, the karakuri dolls that served tea and shot arrows were refined into delicate craft. These figures had no intelligence. They were an art of gears repeating a fixed routine. Even so, people stood before them feeling dread and fascination at once. The simple fact of a human shape moving on its own did that.

The truly hard problem lay elsewhere. Walking. Wheels are easy. Balancing on two legs and putting one foot in front of the other is not. Every step a person takes briefly rests the whole body on one foot, and catches the instability of near-falling with the next. Moving this ordinary act into a machine took humanity decades.

Chapter I

Tokyo, 1973: The First Humanoid

The humanoid in the modern sense was born in 1973 at Waseda University in Japan. It was WABOT-1, built in the laboratory of Professor Ichiro Kato. Standing as tall as a person, this machine walked, very slowly, on two legs, gripped objects with its hands, and held a conversation in Japanese through artificial ears and a mouth. A single step took more than ten seconds, so "walking" was almost a generous word. Still, its place as the first full-scale intelligent humanoid, combining legs, hands, vision, hearing and speech in one body, was clear.

Waseda kept pushing. The WABOT-2 of 1984 read sheet music and played an electronic organ. Its fingers pressed the keys and its feet worked the pedals. The very idea of a machine imitating art was bold for the time. This was the starting point from which Japan would stand at the center of humanoid research for decades.

Why Japan First

There were reasons the country that went first and furthest was Japan. In 1952, Osamu Tezuka's Astro Boy imprinted on a generation of children the idea of the robot not as a frightening monster but as a friend you would want beside you. Some of those children grew up to be engineers. Where the West recalled the terror of Frankenstein when it pictured a robot, Japan imagined a companion.

To this were added the fastest aging society in the world and a formidable precision-manufacturing base. A society losing its workers urgently wanted machines that could help people, and the motor and gear industries that could actually machine such things stood right beside it. A cultural motive and an industrial foundation met in one place.

Chapter II

Honda's Secret Project, and ASIMO

In 1986, Honda quietly began a project no one inside the company would have guessed. A carmaker set out to build a robot that walked like a person. The engineers fixed on a single problem: walking. From the leg-only test machines E0 through E6, they worked out the physics of balance one stage at a time. The key was a concept called ZMP, the zero moment point. Keep the resultant of the forces pressing the sole against the ground always within the foot, and the robot does not fall. The challenge was to compute this principle in real time and control dozens of joints together.

After ten years of silence, in December 1996, Honda revealed P2. Carrying its battery and computer on its back, this 182-centimeter robot walked on its own without a tether, climbed stairs, and recovered its posture when pushed. The robotics world was stunned. While university labs around the world wrestled with small leg models, a single company had built, alone, a self-contained bipedal humanoid close to a finished form. A more refined P3 followed the next year.

In 2000, the culmination arrived. ASIMO. Shrunk to around 130 centimeters to meet people at eye level, it turned smoothly while walking and could even break into a light run. It went up and down stairs, carried a tray, and waved a hand. ASIMO became more than a technology demo; it became the face of Honda. Touring fairs and stages around the world, it imprinted on the public what a humanoid was.

Yet ASIMO had clear limits too. It was strong at performing pre-designed motions gracefully in a controlled environment, but weak at improvising against an unpredictable, rough reality. In the end Honda wound down ASIMO's standalone development in 2018, and after a final demonstration in 2022, sent it off the stage. It was the graceful full stop of an era.

Chapter III

The American Path: From a Leg Lab to Atlas

Across the Pacific, a different lineage was growing. Its starting point was MIT's Leg Laboratory. There, Marc Raibert pushed an approach that caught balance dynamically, by hopping and springing, rather than holding it statically. In 1992 he founded Boston Dynamics and took the work out of the university. Funding from the military and DARPA raised four-legged robots like BigDog, and that motion-control technology eventually moved onto two legs.

The 2011 Fukushima nuclear accident gave this current a direction. What if there had been a robot that could enter a radiation-filled building in place of a person? From that question the DARPA Robotics Challenge (DRC) was launched. Boston Dynamics offered Atlas as the standard platform for the contest. Unveiled in 2013, Atlas was a roughly 1.8-meter hydraulically driven humanoid, designed for disaster-site tasks: shutting valves, opening doors, clearing debris. Its aim, a worker that goes where people cannot rather than a human-like stage performer, set it apart from ASIMO.

Chapter IV

Korea's Leap: HUBO, and the Victory of 2015

It would not be an exaggeration to say Korea's humanoid began with one man's persistence. Professor Jun-Ho Oh of KAIST. His team built up bipedal-walking technology through the KHR series in the early 2000s, and in 2004 finally completed HUBO, Korea's first walking humanoid. The next year they presented Albert HUBO, fitted with an Einstein-like face from America's Hanson Robotics. A head that made expressions and a body that walked met in one machine, an unusual combination for the time.

The real watershed came in June 2015, at the finals of the DARPA Robotics Challenge in Pomona, California. Robots had to solve eight tasks without human help: getting out of a vehicle, opening a door, turning a valve, cutting through a wall, climbing stairs. The world's best teams gathered, and several ran Boston Dynamics' Atlas. The win went to Korea. Professor Oh's Team KAIST, with DRC-HUBO+, completed all eight tasks and reached the top with the fastest time.

The weight of that victory was more than a ranking. A Korean university team had taken first place on the most demanding real-world stage, beating the United States and Japan, long regarded as the home ground of humanoid research. The clever design DRC-HUBO+ showed was striking too. Kneeling down, it rolled quickly on wheels mounted at its legs; standing up, it did precise work on two feet. This idea of switching between walking and rolling as the situation demanded was the fruit of a pragmatism that said a robot need not move exactly like a human.

Epilogue

So Ends Act One

Half a century, from WABOT to ASIMO, from MIT's Leg Laboratory to Atlas, and on to HUBO's victory. The humanoids of this era stood mostly in laboratories, contest grounds and exhibition stages. They were precise but expensive, impressive but slow, and above all weak in the "head" that sees and judges for itself. Following a pre-written motion accurately was the limit.

What broke that limit was the 2020s. Two changes overlapped. Cheap, strong electric drivetrains and batteries pushed aside the heavy and finicky hydraulics, and artificial intelligence finally gave robots usable eyes and judgment. Tesla and Figure, Unitree, and the many names this series will meet in turn rode these two waves. The next part maps the present of the humanoid as it walks onto factory floors and stages this very moment, the lay of the land among the giants.
